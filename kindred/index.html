Long-lost cousins of the English lexicon — each pair below descends from a single ancestor.

About Kindred

Language is a tangled network of traveling sound and meaning. Words arrive through different trajectories. Some don't stray from home, passed down from speaker to speaker for a thousand years. Others descend from the same ancient family, wander through divergent lineages, and arrive back changed beyond recognition. Kindred is a tool for exploring long-lost linguistic cousins, known as doublets to linguists. In English such surprising doublets include cow & beef, guest & host, and word & verb. Presently Kindred supports English and Spanish.

How to read a diagram

The shared ancestor sits at the top, with each arc tracing one word's descent. Solid blue marks clean native inheritance, handed straight down the family line. Grey marks a looser descent: older, across languages, or so reshaped that the exact mechanism is fuzzy (often an ancient borrowing worn smooth by time). Dashed orange marks a clear borrowing, a word taken from another language. A dotted line marks a connection made only at the level of a reconstructed root, real but less direct. Forms marked with * are scholarly reconstructions, not attested words. Hover any point for its meaning. A ⚠ note indicates an origin Wiktionary itself records as uncertain.

How it was made

I've wanted to make a tool like this for years, but only found the time thanks to the extraordinary abilities of LLM coding agents. I directed Claude Code (Fable 5) to search easily available sources, and together we built Kindred over the course of a weekend. The core data come from the crowdsourced etymologies of Wiktionary, extracted as a graph of 4.2 million relationships by etymology-db. An offline pipeline walks that graph for every common word, finds pairs whose ancestries meet, reconstructs both descent chains, and scores each pair for surprise. That surprise score is my own heuristic, rewarding how familiar the two words are, how deep in time they parted, how differently they traveled, and how far their forms and meanings have drifted. The ingredients are public (word frequencies from the wordfreq project, meanings compared with WordNet, definitions from kaikki.org), but the recipe that weighs them is just my own taste for what makes a doublet delightful.
